Preface

Welcome! If you are reading this, chances are you have been asked (or voluntarily chose) to learn R. Either way, you are in the right place.

This book has one job: teach you R from scratch. No prior programming experience needed. If you can use a spreadsheet, you can learn R. It is going to feel weird at first — typing code instead of clicking buttons — but stick with it. The payoff is worth it.

By the end, you will be able to import messy data, clean it up, compute summary statistics, build publication-quality charts, and produce self-updating reports — all in R.

How this book is organized

  • Chapters 1–2: Getting started. Installing R, finding your way around RStudio, and not panicking.
  • Chapters 3–4: Your data analysis toolkit and importing data. Meet the tidyverse and learn how to get data into R from CSVs, Excel files, and databases.
  • Chapters 5–8: Working with data. Describing variables, modifying and reshaping data with dplyr and tidyr.
  • Chapters 9–11: Summary statistics and visualization. Computing the numbers that matter and building charts with ggplot2.
  • Chapters 12–13: Statistical analysis. Hypothesis testing for single variables and relationships between variables.
  • Chapters 14–17: Specialized tools. Working with text (stringr), categories (forcats), dates (lubridate), and repetitive tasks (purrr).
  • Chapters 18–19: R Markdown. Combining your writing and code into polished, self-updating reports, presentations, and dashboards.

A few ground rules

  • You will make errors. Everyone does. The error messages in R look scary, but they are usually telling you something simple, like you forgot a comma. Read them. Google them. You will be fine.
  • Copy-paste is your friend (at first). Run the code in this book. Then change small things and see what happens. That is how you learn.
  • There is no dumb question. If you are confused, someone else is too.

Let’s get started.

Why this book exists at a Jesuit university

You might be wondering what a coding book has to do with Gonzaga’s mission. Fair question. Here is the short answer: everything.

Gonzaga is a Jesuit university, which means we care about educating the whole person — not just the part of you that can pass an exam, but the part that will go out into the world and actually do something meaningful with what you learned. The Jesuit tradition talks about forming “men and women for others,” people who lead with competence and conscience. Data literacy is a big part of that now.

Here is the thing about data: it is never neutral. Every dataset was collected by someone, for some reason, with some set of assumptions baked in. Every summary statistic you compute, every chart you build, every model you deploy — these shape decisions that affect real people. Which neighborhoods get investment. Which patients get treatment. Which applicants get interviews. When you know how to work with data responsibly, you have the power to make those decisions more fair, more transparent, and more grounded in evidence rather than gut feelings.

That is the Jesuit idea of the “common good” in action — not as an abstract ideal you read about in a philosophy class, but as a practical skill you exercise every time you write a line of code.

So yes, this is a book about R. You will learn functions and packages and how to make charts that do not look like they were made in 1997. But underneath all of that is something bigger: the ability to think critically about data, ask whose story it tells (and whose it leaves out), and use your skills in ways that make the world a little more honest and a little more just.

That is why this book exists at a Jesuit university. Now let’s learn some R.
